# Write so the reader understands straight away

Use this guide when writing or rewriting copy. Get the customer's situation, goals and problems from the project context. Use [messaging-and-story.md](messaging-and-story.md) to decide what the message should say.

## Talk about what the customer wants

Start with the person you are talking to: **Identify**. For each message, choose what you want that person to notice: a result they want (**Outcome**) or something they want to avoid (**Objection**). Say that point clearly.

Here is a comparison for track athletes. Each customer message below has its own focus. These are illustrative rewrites, not quotations from the video.

| Focus | Example | What it communicates |
|---|---|---|
| Features only | “Lightweight running shoes made with carbon fabric and nanotechnology.” | What the shoes are made of. The athlete's reason to care is missing. |
| Outcome | “Run 2× faster.” | A result the athlete wants. |
| Objection | “Run without getting blisters.” | An unpleasant experience the athlete wants to avoid. |

The Outcome and Objection examples are separate ways to speak to the same athlete. Choose the point that fits that part of the funnel. Another passage can develop another outcome, such as winning more races, or explain how the shoes help.

Rewriting the feature description as “Light shoes with cushioning” makes the words simpler. It still talks only about the shoes. To write the Story, change the subject to what matters to the athlete.

The same idea applies to software. A tutor who teaches online wants to earn more by taking on more students. Today, they spend their evenings exchanging messages to agree lesson times.

An Outcome message could be:

> Earn €300 more a week by teaching 5 extra students.

An Objection message could be:

> Keep your evenings free from messages about lesson times.

Each message makes one point. The tutor's situation gives both their meaning. A phrase such as “Manage bookings from one dashboard” describes a feature and leaves that meaning unstated.

A later explanation can show how the product helps:

> Students choose from the times you're free and book their own lessons. You get the booking, and they get the lesson link.

This answers the question raised by the Objection message: how can the tutor stop spending their evenings arranging lessons?

## Write as if you were talking to a friend

Use words you would say to someone who knows the situation. Be friendly and direct. The reader should understand the point without translating it into simpler language.

- Choose common words: “get paid,” “book a lesson,” “choose your photos.”
- Say who does what. “Students book their own lessons” is easier to follow than “Enable self-service scheduling.”
- Give each sentence a clear point. Use a longer sentence when it helps explain how two things are connected.
- Use natural contractions such as “you'll,” “it's” and “don't” in English.
- Use technical terms when the audience knows them. Explain what an unfamiliar term means in the situation you are describing.

Short sentences can still be vague. “Do more. Go further. Make it count” gives the reader no clear idea of what they will get. Name the result: “Teach 5 more students each week.”

Use different ways of starting the conversation. For wedding photographers, the result might be delivering the finished album sooner. The problem might be weeks of emails while the couple decides which photos to include.

A question can help the photographer recognize that problem:

> Still waiting for the couple to choose their wedding photos?

A result can give them a reason to look at the offer:

> Deliver the finished wedding album in 7 days, down from 3 weeks.

An explanation can help them understand how it works:

> The couple sees their photos in a private gallery and clicks the ones they want in the album. You receive their complete selection and can start making the album.

The question brings up an Objection: waiting for the couple's decision. The result describes an Outcome: delivering the album sooner. The explanation shows how the product helps. Choose what the reader needs at that point, and let each passage tell them something useful.

Read the copy aloud. If you would have to add “what I mean is…,” put that explanation into the copy.

## Use details that make the change clear

A number can show how much changes, how long something takes or how much time the person gets back. Use digits and readable symbols: `2×`, `50%`, `15 min`, `6 h/week`. Name what they measure.

| Kind of detail | Example |
|---|---|
| Multiplier | “Run 2× faster.” |
| Percentage | “Spend 50% less time arranging lessons.” |
| Time to reach a result | “Deliver the finished wedding album in 7 days.” |
| Before and after | “Deliver the finished wedding album in 7 days, down from 3 weeks.” |
| Time for something the customer wants | “Get 6 h/week back for teaching.” |

Choose a number that helps explain what the customer gains or avoids. “12 integrations” tells them about the product. “6 h/week back for teaching” tells them what they could do with the time they gain.

A situation can make the result clear too. For the tutor, “Finish your last lesson and close your laptop. Tomorrow's students have already booked” shows what an evening free from scheduling messages looks like.

Make the feeling clear through what happens: more students to teach, a finished album delivered to the couple, an evening free from booking messages. The words should let the reader picture the result without having to work out what you mean.

## Background

- [Whop: How to write an offer headline that stops the scroll every time](https://whop.com/blog/how-to-write-offer-headlines/) discusses headlines that focus on benefits, numbers and specific details.
- [Ganz and Grimes: How claim specificity can improve claim credibility in green advertising](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/163071/) found that specific claims were seen as more credible in environmental advertising.
- [Escalas: Imagine yourself in the product](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00913367.2004.10639163) studied how imagining a product experience can affect attitudes towards ads and brands.
